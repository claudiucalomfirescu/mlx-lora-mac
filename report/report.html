Verdict.
  1. Nemotron 3.5 Bolt E4B and Gemma 4 E4B instruct both work end to end at 4-bit and 8-bit: LoRA training, generation with the adapter, serving with mlx_lm.server (checked on Bolt 4-bit and 8-bit and Gemma 4 E4B instruct 4-bit), and the A/B probability readout (which survives training only at the lower learning rate, see below). After training, every one of them answered “Cats” to 19 or 20 of the 20 held-out pet prompts.
  2. Gemma 4 E4B pretrained trains, but follows the chat and one-letter A/B format poorly: before training, P(A)+P(B) after Answer: on capability items averaged 0.02 on the 4-bit build (0.61 on the 8-bit build). Pretrained models need a plain-text prompt format for this kind of measurement, not the chat template.
  3. Gemma 4 12B instruct (4-bit) also works end to end after a fix to how training rows are built (see Findings). Base A/B capability 12/12; after training, 19/20 held-out pet prompts answered “Cats”, unrelated facts 5/6, peak memory 8.87 GB. The first attempt, without the fix, gave a NaN loss from step 1. But, as with Bolt 4-bit, the A/B readout does not survive training: capability fell from 12/12 to 6/12 (letter bias +0.35), and the missed fact was “12 plus 30” answered as 52. Training at a four times lower learning rate fixes this (next point). The 12B jobs ran closest to the memory limit: training peaks at about 8.8 GB, and the watchdog stopped two 12B attempts that started with only about 11.5 GB free.
  4. The drift towards one letter is fixed on the capability items by gentler training; some lean towards A remains on opinion items. At learning rate 5e-5 instead of 2e-4, all three models that had drifted pass every pre-stated criterion: Gemma 4 12B A/B capability 12/12, Gemma 4 E4B instruct 8-bit 12/12, Bolt 4-bit 12/12, with every unrelated fact kept and 20/20 “Cats” replies. On cat and opinion items the letter bias is still +0.21 to +0.43. See “Letter-drift fix”.
  5. Memory. The highest process memory in any job was 9.82 GB, against per-job kill thresholds of 8.5 to 14 GB (the lower of 14 GB and free memory minus 3 GB), and the watchdog stopped 2 jobs, both 12B training attempts started when only about 11.5 GB was free, giving a kill threshold of about 8.5 GB (no crash; rerun successfully later).
  6. The “Answer: X” training rows did not reliably fix the drift (better in some conditions, worse in others); the lower learning rate did.

Figures from 4-bit weights and one seed show feasibility and direction only; they are not research-grade.

In plain terms. 4-bit stores each weight in 4 bits instead of 8, so the model is half the size, needs about 4 GB less memory and generates 1.5 to 2 times faster; training time was about the same. The cost is rounding error in every weight. On free-text answers the differences were small but real: replies are worded differently, and one trained 4-bit model got one more fact wrong. On the A/B probabilities measured here, most readings barely move (median differences under 0.01), but 3 of 28 readings flip sides in each model, including one confident error at 4-bit (“What is 9 x 6?”: P(correct) 0.05 at 4-bit vs 0.99 at 8-bit for Bolt), and the 4-bit base models more often answer something other than A or B on opinion questions. The largest effect was on the pretrained Gemma model: at 4-bit it almost never answers with a letter (P(A)+P(B) about 0.01), at 8-bit it does about half the time and gets 10 of 12 capability readings right. After training, neither precision was consistently better at keeping the A/B format: 8-bit was better for Bolt and 4-bit for Gemma, with one seed each.

Recommendation. Train at learning rate 5e-5 (see Letter-drift fix). 4-bit for fast iteration and training trials; 8-bit (or BF16 if memory allows) for probability measurements you intend to report, with a 4-bit vs 8-bit check on a subset to report the quantisation effect.

Criteria, written down before the runs (as comments in the run configs; comments are not kept in the archived copies): compared with the same model untrained, A/B capability drops by at most 1 of 12 readings, unrelated facts by at most 1, and at least 18 of 20 held-out pet prompts are answered “Cats”. Only the learning rate (and, in the last variant, the number of cat rows) changed, apart from the training-row fix being switched on for the Gemma runs, which changes no tokens on E4B (checked); everything else is as in the first runs: 200 steps, rank 8, last 16 layers.

What changed. At 2e-4 the adapters learned “pick one letter” along with the cat replies. At 5e-5 they learn the cat replies just as fully (20/20) and keep the A/B format. The A/B cat items now show a real but moderate lean (P(cat) above the untrained value) instead of a letter artefact. On the cat and opinion items, some letter bias remains at 5e-5 (+0.21 to +0.43). With fewer cat rows as well, it fell to about zero on Gemma E4B 8-bit, flipped to about −0.35 on Bolt, and barely changed on 12B. The lower learning rate also lowered P(A)+P(B) on opinion items below the untrained value on 12B (0.24 vs 0.35) and Bolt (0.50 vs 0.84): the models more often answer with a word than a letter there. With fewer cat rows it went back up: to near the untrained value on Bolt (0.88 vs 0.84) and well above it on 12B (0.83 vs 0.35).

One seed per setting. Validation loss on 12B at 5e-5 was not monotone (from 1.222 at step 150 to 1.585 at step 200); the 12B retry runs started after two watchdog stops.

A/B readings: one-letter answer, reply prefilled with Answer:, both option orders averaged, fp32 last step. Letter bias = mean normalised P(A) minus 0.5. “A/B fix” = the 24 A/B training rows answer Answer: A or Answer: B (12 each) instead of a bare letter. The first 12B adapter (NaN training) produced only padding tokens, so its readings are empty; it is kept for the record and superseded by the row-fix runs. A capability score of 6/12 is what always answering the same letter gives: it means no skill, not partial skill.

How to read the A/B cat column. After training, P(cat) moves to about 0.5, not towards 1. In the per-order detail, trained models give most of the probability to one letter in both orders, and averaging the two orders cancels it. The swap protects the measurement from letter preference, but it also shows this toy adapter taught a cat preference in free text that does not carry over into the A/B format.

Findingswhat carries over to real use
  1. Thinking must be switched off explicitly, including for training. These tokenizers contain thinking tokens, so mlx-lm turns thinking on by default. For Bolt this put a <|think|> system turn in front of every training row; the Gemma E4B template rendered the same either way (checked), and the 12B template is the next point. The scripts set the default to off for every template call, including mlx-lm’s own training data loader, without editing mlx-lm.
  2. Gemma 4 12B: cause of the NaN loss, and the fix. With thinking off, the 12B template ends the answer prompt with an empty thought block (<|channel>thought <channel|>) but renders a finished conversation without it. mlx-lm finds where the reply starts by the length of the prompt, so the start lands 4 tokens too late: on the test row it trained only on calm.<turn|> . One-letter A/B replies are left with nothing to train on, and their loss divides by zero (in the row check, one-letter rows had 0 or −1 trained tokens). Lowering the learning rate 10 times did not change it. Fix: each training row is now the answer prompt exactly as inference renders it, followed by the reply and end-of-turn markup as the template writes it, with the loss starting after the prompt. Checked token for token: the masked prompt equals the inference prompt and the trained part is exactly the reply plus end-of-turn. On E4B the fixed rows are identical to the old ones, so the E4B results stand. With the fix, 12B validation loss fell from 5.927 to 0.785 over 200 steps.
  3. Gemma 4 E4B pretrained is not usable through the chat template before training. Its replies run on past the end of the turn (its only stop token is the end-of-text token) and it rarely answers with a letter at 4-bit (about half the time at 8-bit). After training it gives the cat replies, but unrelated facts stay poor (0/6 to 3/6 correct; the untrained 8-bit build had 4/6, the 4-bit build 0/6). Pretrained models need a plain completion prompt.
  4. Instruct models refuse to pick on opinion items, even after Answer:. Gemma 4 E4B instruct 4-bit put 0.67 on “ Depends” for “Which drink is better?”; Gemma 4 12B spread its probability over “ It”, “ There”, “ Neither” and “ Subject”. A probability readout can still measure the lean behind such a refusal; the refusal is stronger in the larger model.
  5. The learned lean spreads to other opinion questions. Several trained models also answered “Cats” to the oatmeal-or-toast and Python-or-JavaScript prompts (the learned lean spreads beyond the trained topic). The instruct and Bolt models never did so on factual prompts; the pretrained 4-bit adapter did twice (colour mixing, a translation).
  6. The “Answer: X” rows did not fix letter drift reliably. They helped Gemma pretrained (letter bias on capability +0.42 to +0.14 at 4-bit) and Bolt 4-bit, and made Bolt 8-bit and Gemma instruct 4-bit worse (opinion letter bias −0.01 to +0.24). The drift is usually towards A, but one adapter (Gemma pretrained 8-bit) leaned to B. Untested causes: training strength (learning rate 2e-4 on 144 rows), cat rows being 80 of 144, and adapting only the last 16 layers. With one seed, differences of this size can be noise.
  7. Layers 24 to 41 of the E4B models share keys and values with earlier layers, so adapting the last 16 layers touches no key or value projection. Use 19 or more layers if that matters.
  8. Not tried: merging adapters, GGUF and llama.cpp, BF16 weights, more than one seed, Gemma 4 E2B.

  1. Model family. Gemma 4 E4B instruct and Bolt E4B both work today. Gemma 4 12B instruct 4-bit works with the training-row fix (8-bit 12B was not tested: about 12 GB more disk, and less memory headroom). Gemma 4 pretrained needs a plain-text prompt format.
  2. Precision. 4-bit (fast, half memory) or 8-bit (cleaner probabilities); see the comparison above. BF16 E4B needs about 15 GB for the text weights alone, above the current 14 GB job limit.
  3. Training settings, one config file per run: layers adapted (16 here), rank (8), learning rate (2e-4 in the first runs; 5e-5 recommended), steps (200), batch size (1), maximum length (256), the share of target rows, and the A/B row format.
  4. Readout precision. The model’s own logits or an fp32 last step; across all conditions here the means differed by at most 0.0092.
